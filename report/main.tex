\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

Over the past twenty years, there have been a handful of recurrent cancer
mutations that have rewritten how certain patients are clinically treated.
BRAF V600E moved a significant chunk of metastatic melanomas off cytotoxic
only regimens. EGFR exon 19 deletions and L858R allowed many with NSCLC to
be managed with a daily tablet.

Cancer treatment now incorporates tumor sequencing and other biomarker
assays to identify actionable alterations. Examples include BRAF V600E in
melanoma, EGFR ex19 deletions in NSCLC, KRAS G12C across multiple tumor
types, and HER2 amplification in breast/gaastric cancers. While the promise
of personalized medicine (matched therapy to mutational profile) has not
fully reached its potential and conventional modalities of treatment
(chemo, surgery, immunotherapy) remain bedrocks of cancer care, molecularly
targeted therapies have transformed outcomes for particular subsets of
patients with particular driver alterations. Variant interpretation, as a
result, is at the crux of any clinical workflow, informing trial and
therapeutic eligibility, treatment, and ultimately prognosis.

Evolution set. These indicate that Structure and Evolution set shows
distinct features and correlation with the oncogenicity/pathogenicity of
the variant, and it can potentially be good predictors.

\subsection{Model Choice \& Evaluation}
We chose to evaluate our data on 3 different models to ensure a wide
variety of model types are covered, and the effects of feature set
selection are fully studied. We chose to implement Random Forest,
MultiLayer Perceptron (Neural Network, MLP), and XGBoost. For Random
Forest, we use sklearn's \texttt{RandomForestClassifier} with 300
estimators with no max depth restriction and a minimum sample leaf of 2,
we also include the class weight as balanced \citep{pedregosa2011sklearn}.
For MLP, we use sklearn's \texttt{MLPClassifier} with two hidden layers of
size 32 and 16 respectively and a max iteration of 500
\citep{pedregosa2011sklearn}. And for XGBoost, we use \texttt{XGBClassifier}
from the xgboost library with 100 estimators, max depth of 4, learning rate
of 0.1, \texttt{scale\_pos\_weight} of 1, and log loss as the evaluation
metric \citep{chen2016xgboost}.

\subsection{Class Distribution}
The class label distribution is mildly imbalanced ($\approx$1.4:1
benign:oncogenic), so class balancing techniques are not strictly
necessary. Nonetheless, we applied class reweighting to Random Forest and
XGBoost models, as it is natively supported through \texttt{class\_weight}
and \texttt{scale\_pos\_weight} inputs, while keeping MLP models trained on
natural distribution, as MLPs do not natively support class imbalance. The
impact of class imbalance is expected to be small.

Lift means comparing with best single feature set performance, the
improvement gained. So Sequence + Structure means
$(\textrm{Sequence} + \textrm{Structure}) -
\max(\textrm{Sequence}, \textrm{Structure})$.

\begin{itemize}
  \item All combine yields highest lift in Random Forest, Random forest
        seems to benefit the most from additional features.
  \item Evolution only yields less improvement, it might be expected since
        there's only 2 new features.
\end{itemize}

\noindent``Across all multi-modal combinations and all three models, the
lift over the best constituent single modality was positive ($+0.03$ to
$+0.12$ macro-F1), indicating sequence, structure, and evolutionary
features carry complementary signal.''

\subsection{Feature Lift}
For each multi-modal feature set, we compute
\[
  \mathrm{lift} = \mathrm{macro\_f1}(\textrm{combined}) -
                  \max\bigl(\mathrm{macro\_f1}\ \text{of its constituent
                  single modalities}\bigr).
\]

For each of the 4 multi-modal sets (\texttt{seq\_struct}, \texttt{seq\_evo},
\texttt{struct\_evo}, \texttt{all}), per model:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{seq\_struct} / RF:
        $0.814 - \max(\text{sequence}=0.684, \text{structure}=0.723) =
         0.814 - 0.723 = +0.091$.
  \item \texttt{all} / RF: $0.863 - \max(0.684, 0.723, 0.739) = +0.124$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What positive vs zero lift means.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Lift $> 0$: combining genuinely adds information beyond the best
        single ingredient -- the modalities are complementary.
  \item Lift $\approx 0$: combining gives you nothing you couldn't get
        from just using the best single feature alone (modalities are
        redundant).
  \item Lift $< 0$ (didn't happen in our data): combining hurts -- the
        model is confused by the extra dimensions.
\end{itemize}

A single grouped-bar figure showing all 12 lifts (4 multi-modal sets
$\times$ 3 models) all positive is direct evidence of complementarity.
Without it, a reader could counter ``maybe \texttt{all} is just evolution
doing all the work'' -- feature lift forecloses that interpretation by
subtracting out the best single component.

\paragraph{Discussion Point 2: Different model types affect performance
less as more features are added.}
``The model gap shrinks as feature richness grows. The headline finding is
feature-driven, not model-driven. Across all three model classes, adding
structure and evolutionary features yielded a consistent $+0.17$--$0.20$
macro-F1 improvement, while differences between models within a feature set
were $\leq 0.015$ on the richer feature sets -- within seed-to-seed noise.
Model choice mattered most on the impoverished sequence-only setting,
where MLP lagged tree-based models, suggesting tree models are more robust
under low-signal conditions.''
